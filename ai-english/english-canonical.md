## Tiny concept

The root is canon, from Greek "kanon" meaning a measuring rod or rule. So canonical basically means "according to the rule" — the version everyone agrees is the right or standard one.

It shows up in three contexts you will actually meet:

1. Literature / art / religion — the "canon" is the group of works everyone agrees are important.
   - Shakespeare is a canonical writer.
   - The Bible's canonical books are the ones officially accepted by the Church.

2. Tech / math — the simplest or officially recognized form of something.
   - In SEO, a canonical URL tells Google which version of a page is the "main" one.
   - Mathematicians reduce an equation to its canonical form.

3. General formal use — meaning "orthodox / widely accepted."
   - That interpretation has become canonical among historians.

## A quick note for learners

Canonical sounds fancy, but it just means "the one people treat as standard." Don't overuse it in casual speech — it sits better in writing, articles, and academic contexts. If you say "Pizza Hut's recipe is the canonical way to make pizza," it sounds a bit jokey (and probably wrong). Save it for when there really *is* an agreed standard.
